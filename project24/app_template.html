Our Neighborhood in three dimensions

Every star within three hundred and twenty-six light-years of the Sun. The light from the farthest of them set out around the year 1700, and is arriving now.

In plain words

Each point of light is a real star, placed in 3D where the European Space Agency's Gaia satellite measured it, and painted in its real colour. Distances are in parsecs: one parsec is 3.26 light-years, about 31 trillion kilometres.

01 Here

A small yellow star, one of many.

Lit around the Sun are its nearest neighbours: 3,000 stars within twenty-five parsecs. From the parallax, a distance. From the distance, a place.

In plain words

How do we know how far away a star is? Hold up a finger and look at it with one eye, then the other: it seems to jump against the background. That jump is parallax. As Earth circles the Sun, nearby stars jump the same way against distant ones.

The jump is tiny. For a star 100 parsecs away it is 10 milliarcseconds, about the width of a coin seen from 200 kilometres. Gaia measured it for about 1.5 billion stars. A smaller jump means a farther star.

parallax > 10 milliarcseconds ⟷ distance < 100 parsecs

02 Sphere

215,317

Pull back and the neighborhood becomes a sphere two hundred parsecs across, evenly sown. Lit here are the red dwarfs, 73% of everything. They burn so slowly that not one has yet died.

In plain words

Why a sphere? We kept every star closer than 100 parsecs in every direction, so the selection is a ball with the Sun at its centre. The Milky Way's disc of stars is several hundred parsecs thick, so the whole ball sits inside it and stars fill it evenly.

Red dwarfs are small, cool stars, the most common kind in the Galaxy. Almost none can be seen without a telescope: they are too faint.

red dwarf: colour BP − RP > 1.8

03 Reading starlight

Two numbers tell a star's story.

Colour, and true brightness. Arrange every star by these two, and the scatter of the sky falls into order.

In plain words

Colour tells temperature. Blue-white stars are hot, around 10,000 °C at the surface. Yellow stars like the Sun are about 5,500 °C. Red stars are cooler, near 3,000 °C.

True brightness is how bright a star would look if every star were moved to the same distance (10 parsecs). Astronomers call it absolute magnitude, and smaller numbers mean brighter.

Plot one against the other and you get the Hertzsprung–Russell (HR) diagram, shown at the right. Most stars fall on a diagonal band called the main sequence: stars in the long middle of their lives, burning hydrogen. Where a star sits tells you its stage of life. Our Sun is marked on it.

colour BP − RP = how much brighter a star is in red light than in blue

04 Embers

15,373

White dwarfs. Each is the core a star left behind when it died, cooling for billions of years. They are old enough to have wandered everywhere, and so they are everywhere.

In plain words

On the HR diagram, white dwarfs sit at the lower left. They are hot, so they glow white-blue, but only about the size of Earth, so they give off very little light.

When a star like the Sun runs out of fuel, it puffs away its outer layers and leaves this dense core behind. The Sun will end this way, billions of years from now. White dwarfs come from stars that died long ago, so they are old, and old stars have had time to drift evenly through the neighbourhood.

white dwarf: absolute G > 10 + 2.5 × (BP − RP)

05 Giants

747

Giants. Stars in old age, swollen to many times the Sun's size and tens of times its brightness. Rare, and scattered like lanterns across the whole sphere.

In plain words

Giants sit at the upper right of the HR diagram. They are cool, so they look orange-red, but they are so large that they are very bright overall.

A star swells into a giant when the hydrogen in its core runs out. That stage is short compared with the rest of its life, which is why there are so few.

giant: absolute G < 3.5 and BP − RP > 1.0

06 Family

The Hyades forty-seven parsecs toward Taurus

A knot of stars born together about 650 million years ago, still travelling as one. The nearest star cluster to the Sun.

In plain words

A star cluster is a group of stars born from the same cloud of gas at the same time. They share an age and a distance, which makes clusters a natural test of how stars age.

To make the cluster stand out, only the brightest stars are lit here, and only in a slice of space 24 parsecs thick, so stars in front and behind don't hide it.

centre x −43.9 · y 0.0 · z −18.1 pc
